# How to Make an Offer People Feel Stupid Refusing

Video 4 · Read It Once · narration script (draft 1)
Sources, credited in the description and not spoken: $100M Offers (Alex Hormozi)

## Hook

Pip tutors maths. He's good at it, he's patient, and he charges twenty dollars an hour. And for three months, almost nobody has booked him.

Two streets away, another tutor charges sixty dollars an hour. She has a waiting list.

She isn't three times smarter than Pip. She doesn't have a fancier degree. The difference is not the tutor. It's the offer.

By the end of this video, you'll know the simple formula behind offers that people feel silly turning down, and you'll be able to use it on anything you sell, from a service to a side project to yourself in a job interview.

## People don't buy hours

Let's start with what Pip was actually selling.

His offer was one hour of maths tutoring, for twenty dollars. That sounds reasonable. But look at it from a parent's side of the table.

The parent doesn't want an hour of maths. Nobody wakes up wanting an hour of maths. What they want is a child who stops crying over homework. A better grade on the report card. A little less worry at the dinner table.

Pip was selling his time. The other tutor was selling a result. And people will always pay more for a result than for time, because time is the cost, and the result is the thing they actually want.

## The value equation

So how do you make a result feel valuable? There's a simple way to think about it, with four parts.

Two parts make an offer feel bigger. The first is the dream outcome, which is how good the result is. The second is how likely it feels that the result will actually happen.

And two parts make an offer feel smaller. The first is how long the buyer has to wait. The second is how much effort and sacrifice it takes from them.

So the value of any offer goes up when the result is bigger, and more certain, and it goes up again when the wait is shorter, and the effort is lower.

Most people only work on the first part. They try to promise a bigger result. But the other three parts are often where the real gains are hiding.

## Pip rebuilds his offer

Let's rebuild Pip's offer, one part at a time.

First, the dream outcome. Pip stops talking about hours. His new offer is about raising a child's grade by one full level in a single school term.

Second, likelihood. Parents have been burned before, so Pip shows proof. He shares the before and after grades of three past students, with their parents' permission. Suddenly, the result doesn't feel like a hopeful promise. It feels like something that has already happened, more than once.

Third, the waiting time. A term is a long time to wait for good news. So every Friday, Pip sends parents a short update with one thing that improved that week. The big result is still months away, but the parents see progress from the first week.

And fourth, effort. Parents are tired and busy. So Pip comes to their home, brings every worksheet himself, and handles the homework battles so the parents don't have to.

Same tutor. Same maths. But the offer is now a completely different thing.

## Remove the risk

There's one more piece that makes an offer very hard to refuse. A guarantee.

The biggest fear in any purchase is the fear of paying and getting nothing. So Pip takes that fear away. If the grade hasn't improved by the end of the term, the next month of tutoring is free.

Pip isn't being reckless. He knows his results. And because he knows them, the guarantee costs him very little, while it removes the biggest reason parents were hesitating.

When a buyer takes no risk, saying no starts to feel like the risky choice.

## The price stops mattering

Now Pip raises his price to sixty dollars an hour.

Here's the surprising part. More parents say yes, not fewer. Because nobody is comparing sixty dollars to twenty dollars any more. They're comparing sixty dollars to a child who's happier at school, with weekly proof, no effort from them, and no risk.

That's what an offer people feel stupid refusing really is. It isn't a trick, and it isn't a lower price. It's an offer where the value is so clearly bigger than the cost that saying no feels like losing money.

## Using it, and spotting it

You can use this on almost anything. Before you sell a product, a service, or even yourself in an interview, run through the four parts. Check that the result is clear and big, that there's proof it's likely, that the wait feels short, and that the effort for the other person is small. Then look for a way to take away their risk.

And the same formula works in reverse when you're the buyer. When an offer feels too good to refuse, check each part calmly. Make sure the proof is real, and that the guarantee is written down. Good offers survive that check. Bad ones usually don't.

## Close

Three months later, Pip has a waiting list of his own.

He didn't become a better tutor overnight. He just stopped selling his hours, and started selling what parents actually wanted, with the risk taken away.

So here's what to remember. People pay for results, not time. Make the result big and believable, make the wait feel short, and make the effort small. And remove the risk, so saying no feels like the bigger mistake.
