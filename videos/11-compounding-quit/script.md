# Why Most People Quit Before Compounding Pays Off

## Hook

Pip starts investing a hundred dollars a month. He's excited. He checks his account every week.

After two years, he has put in two thousand four hundred dollars. His account shows a little over two thousand six hundred.

Two years of discipline, and the reward is about two hundred dollars. Pip decides investing is a waste of time, and he stops.

What Pip didn't know is that he quit at the most boring part of a story that ends very differently. In this video, you'll learn how compounding really works, and why almost everyone gives up right before it pays off.

## The curve nobody expects

Compounding means your money earns money, and then that money earns money too.

At the start, this is almost invisible. The growth is so small it feels like nothing is happening.

But the curve doesn't stay flat. Each year, the growth is added to a bigger pile than the year before. So the line bends. Slowly at first, then faster, and then much faster.

If Pip had kept going with the same hundred dollars a month, at a steady average return, the first ten years would have felt slow. But somewhere after year twenty, his account would grow more in a single year than he had put in during the first ten.

Most of the money arrives at the end. That is the whole point, and it is also the whole problem.

## Time does the heavy lifting

Here's what surprises most people.

The most important number in compounding isn't how much you invest, or even how good your returns are. It's how long you keep going.

Someone with average returns who stays invested for forty years will usually end up far ahead of someone with brilliant returns who stops after ten.

Many of the wealthiest investors in history made most of their money after the age of sixty. Not because they got smarter late in life, but because they started early and never stopped.

## Why people quit

So why do so many people stop?

The first reason is that our brains think in straight lines. We expect ten years of effort to give ten times the result of one year. Compounding doesn't work that way, so the early years feel like failure.

The second reason is boredom. Compounding is slow and quiet. There is no exciting moment, no big win to talk about. People drift towards things that feel faster.

The third reason is fear. When the market falls, the account drops, and it feels like the money is disappearing. Many people sell at exactly the wrong time, and reset their clock back to zero.

## How to stay in long enough

So how do you get to the steep part of the curve?

The first move is to automate it. Set the investment to happen on payday, so it doesn't depend on how you feel that month.

The second move is to check less. Looking at your account every day makes every small drop feel like a crisis. Looking once or twice a year shows you the real direction.

The third move is to expect the drops. Falls are the price of the long term growth, not a sign that something is broken.

And the fourth move is to measure in decades, not months. The question is never what happened this year. It's whether you're still in the game.

## Close

Let's go back to Pip.

A few years later, he starts again. This time, the money moves on payday. He checks his account twice a year. When the market drops, he leaves it alone.

The early years still feel slow. But now he knows that slow is what the start of the curve looks like.

So here's what to remember. Compounding hides its rewards at the end. Time matters more than talent. And the people who win aren't the ones who pick the best investments. They're the ones who don't quit.
