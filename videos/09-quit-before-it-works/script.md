# Why You Quit Just Before It Works

## Hook

Pip starts learning the guitar. He practises every evening for six weeks.

At the end of six weeks, he still sounds terrible. His fingers hurt, the songs still don't sound like songs, and he can't see any progress at all.

So he puts the guitar in the corner, and it stays there.

What Pip didn't know is that he quit right before the part where it starts to work. In this video, you'll learn why progress feels invisible at the start, and how to keep going long enough to see it.

## The ice cube

Imagine an ice cube on a table, in a cold room.

The room starts to warm up, one degree at a time. The cube doesn't change. Another degree, and still nothing. Another degree, and the cube looks exactly the same.

Then the room crosses one more degree, and the ice starts to melt.

That final degree didn't do all the work. Every degree before it did. They were just invisible, because nothing on the outside changed until the very end.

## The valley of disappointment

Most people expect progress to be a straight line. Put in a week of effort, and see a week of results.

But real progress usually looks different. For a long time, results stay flat, far below what you expected. And the gap between what you expected and what you see feels like failure.

That gap is where most people quit. Not because the habit wasn't working, but because the results hadn't surfaced yet.

The work isn't wasted. It's stored. Like the warmth in the room before the ice melts.

## Why it feels so slow

There's a reason the start is so frustrating.

Small improvements are almost impossible to notice day to day. Getting one percent better today looks exactly the same as not improving at all. But those small gains build on each other. And after enough of them, they suddenly add up to something you can see.

This is true for fitness, for savings, for learning a skill, and for building anything worth having. The early stage always looks like nothing is happening.

## How to keep going

So how do you get through the flat part?

The first move is to expect it. Before you start anything new, tell yourself that the first weeks will look like failure. When it happens, it's not a sign to stop. It's a sign that you're in the normal part.

The second move is to track the habit, not the result. Put a mark on a calendar every day you show up. The result may be invisible, but a chain of marks is proof that the work is building.

The third move is to never miss twice. Missing one day is normal. Missing two in a row is the start of a new habit, the habit of not doing it.

And the fourth move is to judge yourself by direction, not distance. If you're doing the work, you're moving the right way, even when the finish line still looks far away.

## Close

Let's go back to Pip.

A few months later, he picks up the guitar again. This time, he expects the flat part. He marks a calendar every evening he practises.

Week six comes, and it still sounds rough. He keeps going. Then, somewhere around week ten, a song finally sounds like a song.

Nothing magical happened that week. All the earlier weeks just finally showed up.

So here's what to remember. Progress is stored before it is seen. The valley of disappointment is where most people quit. And if you keep showing up through the flat part, the results arrive all at once.
