# The 2-Minute Rule: How to Start Any Habit

## Hook

Pip has a plan. Every single January, the same plan. This year, he's going to get fit.

Day one, he runs five kilometres. He feels like a hero. Day two, his legs hurt, but he runs anyway. Day three, it rains. Day four, he's tired. By day nine, the running shoes are back in the closet, and Pip is on the couch, telling himself he'll start again on Monday.

Does that sound familiar?

Here's the strange part. Pip isn't lazy. He wanted this. He just made the same mistake almost everyone makes. He started big.

In this video you'll learn one tiny rule that fixes that. It takes two minutes. And it's the reason small habits quietly beat big plans.

## Why big starts fail

Let's look at what actually happened to Pip.

On day one, he was full of motivation. Motivation feels powerful, but it's like a sugar rush. It spikes, and then it's gone. And a habit has to survive the days when motivation isn't there.

On day four, Pip wasn't asking, "Do I want to be fit?" He was asking, "Do I want to run five kilometres right now, in the rain, when I'm tired?" And the honest answer to that question is almost always no.

That's the real problem. The hard part of a habit isn't doing it. The hard part is starting it. Once Pip is outside and moving, the run is easy enough. It's the getting out the door that he can't face.

So if starting is the hard part, what if we made starting almost impossible to fail?

## The rule

Here's the rule. When you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do.

That's it. Not "run five kilometres." Instead, "put on my running shoes." Not "read a chapter every night." Instead, "read one page." Not "write for an hour." Instead, "write one sentence." Not "meditate for twenty minutes." Instead, "take three slow breaths."

It sounds almost silly. Pip would be standing in his living room, wearing running shoes, and calling that a workout. But watch what happens.

## Why it works

There are three reasons this tiny trick is so powerful.

The first reason: it removes the fight. Putting on shoes takes thirty seconds. Pip can't talk himself out of that. There's no rain big enough, and no tiredness deep enough, to make thirty seconds feel like too much.

The second reason: starting is the hardest part, and you've just done it. Pip stands there, shoes on, door right in front of him. And most days, he thinks, "Well, I'm here already." So he goes for a short jog. Some days he runs more than he planned. But even on the days he doesn't, he still showed up.

And that's the third reason, and the most important one. Every habit is really a vote for the kind of person you are. When Pip puts on his shoes, he casts a vote for "I'm a person who exercises." One vote isn't much. But a hundred votes is an identity. After three months of showing up, Pip doesn't have to push himself. It's simply who he is now.

## The mistake people make

Now, you might be thinking: "But two minutes of anything won't change my life."

And you're right. Two minutes of reading won't make you well read. Two minutes of exercise won't change your body. That isn't the point.

The two-minute rule is not the finish line. It's the front door. The goal is to master the habit of showing up. Because a habit has to be established before it can be improved. You can't get better at something you don't do.

Think of it as a staircase. Step one is the two-minute version. Once that feels automatic, you make it a little bigger. Five minutes. Then ten. Pip goes from shoes on, to one lap around the block, to a short run, and eventually to the five kilometres he tried to do on day one. Only this time, it lasts. Because he built it from the bottom, instead of throwing himself off the top.

## How to use it today

Here's how to use this today.

Step one. Pick one habit you've failed at before. Just one.

Step two. Shrink it until it takes less than two minutes. If you're not sure it's small enough, make it smaller. The version you're embarrassed by is probably the right one.

Step three. Attach it to something you already do. After I pour my morning coffee, I put on my shoes. After I brush my teeth, I read one page. Your existing routines become the trigger, so you don't have to remember.

And step four. Stay small for at least two weeks. This is the hard bit, because you'll feel like you could do more. Resist it. For now, the only job is to show up every single day.

## Close

Let's go back to Pip.

It's January again. This time there's no big plan. Just a pair of running shoes by the front door. After his morning coffee, he puts them on. Some days that's all he does. Some days he runs around the block. Once in a while he goes much further than he meant to.

By March, he hasn't missed a week. Nobody gave him a medal. Nobody noticed. But Pip noticed. He's become someone who shows up.

So here's what to remember. Starting is the hard part, so make starting tiny. Show up first, and improve later. And small habits aren't a smaller version of big goals. They're how big goals actually get built.
