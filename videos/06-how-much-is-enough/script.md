# How Much Money Is Enough?

Video 6 · Read It Once · narration script (draft 1)
Sources, credited in the description and not spoken: The Psychology of Money (Morgan Housel)

---

## Hook

A few years ago, Pip earned three thousand dollars a month. He was sure of one thing. If he could just earn five thousand, he would finally feel relaxed.

Then he got to five thousand. For about two months, it felt amazing. Then a friend bought a bigger flat, a colleague got a new car, and suddenly five thousand felt ordinary. Now the magic number was eight.

Pip's income went up by more than half, and he felt exactly as stressed as before.

This is one of the most common money traps there is, and it has nothing to do with how much you earn. By the end of this video, you'll know why the finish line keeps moving, and how to set one that stays put.

## The moving goalpost

Here's what happened to Pip.

Every time his income rose, his expectations rose with it. Better food became normal food. A nicer flat became just home. And whatever is normal stops feeling like a reward.

So the extra money never felt like extra. It felt like the new floor. And the goal he was chasing simply moved forward, one step ahead of him, every time he got close.

This is why so many people who earn far more than they used to still feel behind. They didn't fail to reach the goal. The goal kept running away.

## The ladder with no top

There's a second force that makes this worse. Comparison.

Pip doesn't measure his money against what he needs. He measures it against the people around him. And there is always someone further up.

When he earns more, he moves into new circles, and those circles have bigger flats, nicer holidays and newer cars. So he climbs a rung, looks up, and sees exactly the same distance to the top as before.

The problem isn't that Pip is greedy. It's that comparison is a ladder with no top. If enough means more than the people around you, then enough can never arrive.

## The real danger

Wanting more can feel harmless. But it hides a real danger.

When nothing is ever enough, people start taking risks they don't need to take. They borrow to keep up. They chase a get-rich-quick investment. They put their savings, their health or their reputation on the line, just to reach a number they will outgrow anyway.

There are stories of people with fortunes most of us can't imagine, who still wanted more, broke the rules to get it, and lost their freedom and their good name. They had everything they needed, and risked it for something they didn't need at all.

That is the core lesson. Never risk what you have and need, for what you don't have and don't need.

## How to find your enough

So how do you set a finish line that stays put?

The first step is to write down what a good life actually costs you. Not a dream life, and not your neighbour's life. Your rent, your food, your savings, a few things that make you genuinely happy. That number is usually much smaller than people expect.

The second step is to notice when the number moves because of someone else. If you only wanted something after you saw it on another person, that wish belongs to them, not you.

The third step is to decide what you will never risk. Your health, your family, your freedom, your reputation, and the money that keeps you safe. Whatever the opportunity, those stay off the table.

And the fourth step is to let your savings grow when your income grows, instead of letting your lifestyle quietly eat the difference.

## Close

Let's go back to Pip.

He sat down one evening and wrote out what a good month actually cost him. It came to a lot less than eight thousand. In fact, he was already earning it.

Nothing about his income changed that night. But for the first time in years, Pip felt rich. Not because he had more, but because he finally knew what enough looked like.

So here's what to remember. The goalpost moves every time you get close, so you have to fix it yourself. Comparison is a ladder with no top. And never risk what you need for something you don't.
