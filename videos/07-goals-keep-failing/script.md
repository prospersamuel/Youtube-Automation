# Why Your Goals Keep Failing (and What to Set Instead)

## Hook

Every January, Pip sets a goal. This year, he wants to save five thousand dollars by December.

He writes it on a sticky note and puts it on his fridge. He feels motivated. He feels ready.

By March, the sticky note has fallen behind the fridge, and his savings account looks exactly the way it did in January.

Pip isn't bad at goals. He just put all his attention on the wrong thing. In this video, you'll learn why most goals quietly fail, and what to set instead, so this year actually ends differently.

## Everyone has the same goal

Here's something strange about goals.

Think about everyone who wants to save more money this year. Millions of people set that exact goal. The ones who succeed and the ones who fail wanted the same thing.

So the goal can't be what made the difference. If winners and losers share the same goal, something else is deciding who wins.

That something else is the system. The goal is the result you want. The system is what you actually do, every day, to move towards it.

## Three problems with goals

Goals are not useless. They give you direction. But on their own, they come with three problems.

The first problem is that a goal is a single moment. Once you hit it, or once you miss it, it's over. Pip's goal only cares about one day in December. It says nothing about the other three hundred and sixty four.

The second problem is that goals delay happiness. Pip tells himself he'll feel good once he has five thousand saved. Until then, every month feels like failing. And when people feel like they're failing, they quit.

The third problem is the bounce back. Plenty of people push hard to hit a goal, then relax once they reach it, and slide right back to where they started. The goal ended, so the effort ended too.

## You fall to the level of your system

There's a simple way to put it. You don't rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems.

When motivation fades, and it always does, the goal stops pulling you forward. What's left is whatever you do automatically. If there's no system underneath the goal, there's nothing left at all.

That's what happened to Pip in March. His goal was still on the fridge. But nothing in his daily life had actually changed.

## What to set instead

So let's rebuild Pip's year around a system.

First, he moves the decision to payday. On the day his salary arrives, a fixed amount moves into savings automatically, before he can spend it. He doesn't have to be motivated. It just happens.

Second, he picks one small daily behaviour. He cooks dinner at home four nights a week. It's not dramatic. But it's specific, and it's something he can do today.

Third, he measures the system, not the goal. Instead of asking how far he is from five thousand, he asks a simpler question each week, whether he stuck to the system or not. That's a question he can answer with a yes almost every time.

And fourth, he keeps the goal, but moves it to the back seat. It sets the direction. The system does the driving.

## Close

Let's go back to Pip.

It's December again. He never thought about the sticky note. He just let payday do its job, and cooked at home most nights.

When he finally checks his savings account, the number is higher than five thousand. He didn't chase it. He just built a system that got there on its own.

So here's what to remember. Winners and losers usually have the same goals. You fall to the level of your system. And the best goal is one you never have to think about, because your system already takes care of it.
