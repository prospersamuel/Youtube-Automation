# Why You're Still Broke on a Good Salary

Video 1 · Read It Once · narration script (draft 2)
Sources, credited in the description and not spoken: The Psychology of Money (Morgan Housel), Rich Dad Poor Dad (Robert Kiyosaki)

---

## Hook

Meet Prosper. Prosper just got a raise. A big one. He goes from three thousand dollars a month to four thousand. He's thrilled. This is it. This is when life finally gets easier.

Six months later, it's the twenty-sixth of the month. Prosper opens his banking app, and there's eleven dollars in it.

Exactly like before the raise.

So where did an extra thousand dollars a month go? Nobody robbed him. He didn't gamble it. And he's not bad at maths.

Prosper's problem is the same one millions of people with good salaries have. And it has almost nothing to do with how much he earns.

He has walked into three money traps. By the end of this video you'll know which ones you're in, and the three moves that get you out.

## Trap one: the raise that disappears

Let's rewind to the week of the raise.

Prosper looks around his small apartment and thinks: I earn more now. I deserve better. So he moves somewhere nicer. That's four hundred dollars more a month.

Then he looks at his old car. It works fine. But it doesn't look like the car of someone who earns four thousand a month. So he gets a newer one. That's a three hundred and fifty dollar payment.

Then come the small things. Better restaurants. A few more subscriptions. Nicer clothes. Another two hundred and fifty.

Add it up. Four hundred, plus three fifty, plus two fifty. That's exactly one thousand dollars. The entire raise, gone, before he saved a single dollar of it.

This is the rat race. You earn more, so you spend more, so you need to earn more. You run faster, but you stay in the same place.

And the scary part is that it never feels like overspending. Every upgrade felt reasonable. It just felt like living the life he had earned.

So here is the first hard truth. A raise does not fix a spending problem. It just gives the problem a bigger budget.

## Trap two: the show

But why did Prosper upgrade everything in the first place? His old apartment kept the rain out. His old car got him to work.

Be honest. A lot of it wasn't for him. It was for the audience.

A writer who once parked cars at a fancy hotel made a brilliant observation about this. He watched people pull up in Ferraris and Lamborghinis every day. And he noticed something strange.

When he saw someone in an incredible car, he almost never thought, "wow, that driver is impressive." He thought, "wow, if I had that car, people would think I'm impressive."

Think about what that means. Nobody is admiring the driver. They're imagining themselves in the seat.

So the things we buy to earn respect barely work. People are too busy thinking about themselves to be impressed by you. You pay full price for the admiration, and it never arrives.

Prosper is spending three hundred and fifty dollars a month on a car, to impress people who never looked up from their phones.

## Trap three: rich is not the same as wealthy

This leads to the idea that changed how I see money more than any other.

Wealth is what you don't see.

Think about it. When you see someone driving a hundred thousand dollar car, the only thing you know for certain about their money is that they have a hundred thousand dollars less than they used to. Or a hundred thousand dollars more debt.

Being rich is about the money you earn and spend. It's visible. The car. The watch. The holiday photos.

Being wealthy is the opposite. Wealth is the money you didn't spend. It's the car you didn't buy, and the upgrade you skipped. It sits quietly in savings and investments, where nobody can see it.

Picture two neighbours. The first drives a brand new car, wears a different outfit every weekend, and posts every holiday. The second drives a ten year old car, and almost never talks about money. Ask anyone on the street which one is doing better, and they'll point at the first.

But the first neighbour owes money on the car, the clothes and the holidays. The second owns everything outright, and has been quietly investing for ten years. One of them looks rich. The other one is wealthy. And from the street, you can't tell the difference.

And that creates a problem. We learn about money by copying what we see. But the only thing we can see is the spending. We can't see anyone's bank balance. So we copy the spending, and assume the wealth comes with it.

It doesn't. Plenty of people who look rich are one missed paycheck away from trouble. And plenty of people in ordinary cars could stop working tomorrow.

## Quick test

Before we fix it, here's a quick test.

If your income has gone up in the last few years, but your savings haven't, you're in trap one.

If you've ever bought something mainly so that other people would see it, that's trap two.

And if you judge how well someone is doing by what they drive, that's trap three.

Most of us are in all three. I was.

## The way out

So what should Prosper do? Here are three moves. None of them need a bigger salary.

Move one. Pay yourself first.

Most people spend first, and save whatever is left. And whatever is left is usually nothing. So flip the order. The day your salary arrives, a fixed slice goes straight into savings, automatically, before you can touch it. Then you live on the rest. You'll be surprised how quickly you adjust. You can't spend what you never see.

Start small if you have to. Even five percent is fine. The amount matters less than the habit. Once the habit exists, raising the number is easy.

Move two. When your income goes up, don't let your lifestyle follow at the same speed.

The next time you get a raise, let half of it improve your life, and send the other half to your savings. The logic is simple. You can build wealth without a high income. But you have almost no chance of building it without a high savings rate. And the gap between what you earn and what you spend is the one part of money you fully control.

Move three. Buy assets before liabilities.

This is one of the most famous money lessons ever written, and it's almost too simple. An asset puts money into your pocket. A liability takes money out of your pocket.

A share in a company that pays you dividends. A small business. Even a skill that raises your income. Those are assets.

A car payment. The newest phone, bought on credit. A bigger apartment than you need. Those are liabilities.

Most people buy liabilities and call them rewards. The wealthy buy assets first, and then let the assets pay for the rewards.

So it's not that you can never have the nice car. It's about the order. Assets first. Toys later, paid for by the assets.

## Close

Now imagine Prosper had known all this the week he got his raise.

Same job. Same four thousand dollars. But on payday, five hundred disappears into savings and investments before he ever sees it. He keeps the car that works. He stays in his apartment a little longer.

On the twenty-sixth of the month he opens his banking app. And for the first time, the number is going up.

Nobody on the street can tell. He doesn't look any richer. And that is exactly the point.

Because the best thing money buys isn't a car, or a watch, or other people's opinions. The highest form of wealth is being able to wake up and say, "I can do whatever I want today." Money's greatest value is control over your own time.

So here's what to remember. A raise won't save you if your spending rises with it. Nobody is as impressed by your stuff as you think. And real wealth is what you don't see.
